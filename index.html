A conversation starter, not a test

Mind the Gap

Most of the time, when one person feels unseen, it isn’t because the other isn’t trying. It’s because the trying isn’t arriving. This tool measures both halves of that — what one of you believes you give, and what the other actually experiences — and shows you the distance between them.

Do this together

Start a shared session and you’ll get a link to send your partner. You each answer two short sets — one about them, one about yourself — whenever suits, and the comparisons appear as the pieces arrive. No accounts, no names.

The session deletes itself after 30 days. Anyone holding the link can see it, so send it only to them.

How it works

  1. You each answer 18 questions, separately, about four minutes each.
  2. One of you answers about the other — how understood and valued you feel. The other answers about themselves — how well they think they do those things.
  3. The two readings are compared, so you can see where what’s given and what’s felt diverge.

Both of you are describing the same person’s responsiveness, from the inside and the outside. Do it in both directions to see the full picture.

What this is, and what it isn’t

The questions are the Perceived Partner Responsiveness Scale, a published research instrument. It measures a perception — your belief about how another person sees you. Reis, Clark and Holmes, who developed the underlying theory, are careful about this: these perceptions contain a real “kernel of truth,” but they are also shaped by mood, history and how secure you feel in general. A low score is not proof that someone is failing you, and a high one is not proof that they aren’t.

There are no norms here on purpose. The published papers report reliability and correlations, but no population averages or percentile bands — so this tool will never tell you that you scored “above average” or “in the bottom quarter.” Anyone who shows you those numbers for this scale made them up. What you get instead is where your answers sit on the scale you just used, which is the honest version.

Expectations are also relative. The same act reads as neglectful from a spouse and thoughtful from a neighbour — the theory calls this communal strength. You are answering about one specific relationship, with all the expectations that come with it.

This is not a diagnostic instrument, not therapy, and not a verdict on a relationship. If it surfaces something heavy, that is a sign to talk to each other or to a professional — not to trust a number.

What to do with this

The useful question is almost never “who’s right.” How it’s felt is a real experience, even when it doesn’t match what’s being given. A self-rating is a real sense of effort, even when it’s generous. So the conversation worth having is what would this look like if it landed?, for the two or three biggest gaps only.

Be specific about what works, not just about what doesn’t. In one interview study, the women whose desire held up over a long relationship more often described being comfortable telling their partner what worked for them (Murray, Milhausen & Sutherland, 2014). It’s a different topic, but the same skill.

The readings and questions on this page are this tool’s suggestions to help the conversation along. They aren’t findings from the research.

Where this comes from, and its limits

Why it sits next to responsiveness. In a 42-day diary study of couples, on days people felt their partner was more responsive, they also felt more desire for them, and that held for men and women about equally. Feeling special, and seeing the partner as a good catch, carried most of that link.

Why it’s gender-neutral. Interviews with women and a survey of men turned up much the same things: being told you’re wanted, touch that isn’t leading anywhere, how sex gets started, and being able to say what you want. The clearest difference was about starting things: men more often wanted their partner to start more, women more often wanted it started better. So that question is asked both ways.

What it isn’t. Part one’s two items come from a daily diary measure with good reliability (α = .83), but they are not a full validated scale, and they were written for the receiving side only. The giving-side wording is this tool’s mirror of them and has never been tested. Part two is drawn from small interview and survey studies, so it is a set of questions, not a measurement. All of the studies were of heterosexual couples, mostly young and mostly white. Your answers in this tab are not saved anywhere.
