A conversation starter, not a test

Mind the Gap

Most of the time, when one person feels unseen, it isn’t because the other isn’t trying. It’s because the trying isn’t arriving. This tool measures both halves of that — what one of you intends, and what the other actually experiences — and shows you the distance between them.

Do this together

Start a shared session and you’ll get a link to send your partner. You each answer two short sets — one about them, one about yourself — whenever suits, and the comparisons appear as the pieces arrive. No accounts, no names.

The session deletes itself after 30 days. Anyone holding the link can see it, so send it only to them.

How it works

  1. You each answer 18 questions, separately, about four minutes each.
  2. One of you answers about the other — how understood and valued you feel. The other answers about themselves — how well they think they do those things.
  3. The two readings are compared, so you can see where intent and experience diverge.

Both of you are describing the same person’s responsiveness, from the inside and the outside. Do it in both directions to see the full picture.

What this is, and what it isn’t

The questions are the Perceived Partner Responsiveness Scale, a published research instrument. It measures a perception — your belief about how another person sees you. Reis, Clark and Holmes, who developed the underlying theory, are careful about this: these perceptions contain a real “kernel of truth,” but they are also shaped by mood, history and how secure you feel in general. A low score is not proof that someone is failing you, and a high one is not proof that they aren’t.

There are no norms here on purpose. The published papers report reliability and correlations, but no population averages or percentile bands — so this tool will never tell you that you scored “above average” or “in the bottom quarter.” Anyone who shows you those numbers for this scale made them up. What you get instead is where your answers sit on the scale you just used, which is the honest version.

Expectations are also relative. The same act reads as neglectful from a spouse and thoughtful from a neighbour — the theory calls this communal strength. You are answering about one specific relationship, with all the expectations that come with it.

This is not a diagnostic instrument, not therapy, and not a verdict on a relationship. If it surfaces something heavy, that is a sign to talk to each other or to a professional — not to trust a number.

This is the sharp end. It lists, in order, the places where one person’s sense of what they give is furthest from what the other receives. It’s useful material for a conversation and a rough thing to read alone.

A positive gap means it’s believed to be given more than it’s felt. A negative gap means more is landing than is being claimed.

What to do with this

The useful question is almost never “who’s right.” Both numbers are real: one person really is trying that hard, and the other really doesn’t feel it. The gap is about form, not effort — so the conversation worth having is what would this look like if it landed?, item by item, for the two or three biggest gaps only.
